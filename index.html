About the workshop

AI has become a systems problem. Training and serving today's models is limited less by algorithms than by the machines and networks underneath them: interconnect topology, congestion, scheduling, memory and energy.

NetSysAI brings together researchers and industry engineers working at that boundary, from datacenter fabrics and self-adjusting networks to efficient models and hardware-aware learning, for a day of talks and discussion in the Negev.

  • Datacenter fabrics & AI interconnects
  • Self-adjusting & reconfigurable networks
  • Congestion control & collective communication
  • Scheduling for training & inference
  • Memory- and energy-efficient AI systems
  • Efficient models & hardware-aware learning

Talks & abstracts

Stefan Schmid · TU Berlin Keynote · Abstract · Bio From Topology-Aware to Fabric-Adaptive: Rethinking Datacenter Networks for AI

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is transforming the way we design datacenter networks. As AI clusters scale to thousands of accelerators, their performance increasingly depends on communication, and in particular on collective operations such as AllReduce and All-to-All. At the same time, modern datacenter fabrics offer capabilities that conventional collective communication does not yet fully exploit: multiple simultaneous communication ports, heterogeneous interconnects, and increasingly, dynamically reconfigurable optical connectivity.

In this talk, I will argue that exploiting these capabilities requires us to move from topology-aware to fabric-adaptive collective communication. I will first discuss multiport networks and present Trivance, a recent approach to latency-efficient AllReduce that systematically exploits parallel communication opportunities. I will then turn to reconfigurable optical networks, where an even more fundamental opportunity arises: rather than merely optimizing communication over a given topology, we can jointly adapt the communication schedule and the network topology itself.

These results point toward a broader vision for AI datacenters in which collective communication and the underlying network are no longer optimized independently. Instead, the network becomes an active resource that adapts to the communication structure of AI workloads. I will discuss recent progress toward this vision, the algorithmic and systems challenges that remain, and opportunities for a new generation of adaptive AI datacenter fabrics.

About the speaker

Stefan Schmid is a Professor at the Technical University of Berlin, Germany. MSc and PhD at ETH Zurich, Postdoc at TU Munich and University of Paderborn, Senior Research Scientist at T-Labs in Berlin, Associate Professor at Aalborg University, Denmark, Full Professor at the University of Vienna, Austria, and sabbatical as a Fellow at the Israel Institute for Advanced Studies (IIAS), Israel. Stefan Schmid received the IEEE Communications Society ITC Early Career Award 2016, an ERC Consolidator Grant 2019, and an ERC Proof-of-Concept Grant 2026. Chaim Baskin · BGU · Decart Abstract · Bio What to Keep and How to Remember: Towards Efficient World Models

Abstract

Interactive world models must do more than generate plausible visual sequences. They must preserve information across time, recover relevant details when needed, and remain computationally efficient as interactions grow longer. Keeping the entire history in context is costly, while compressing it risks discarding information needed later. This raises a fundamental question: what should a model remember, and how can it use that information efficiently?

In this talk, I will explore how learning what to preserve and what to compute can address these challenges. I will present recent work on compact visual representations for efficient video retrieval, learned spatial memory for consistent generation across long trajectories, and phase-aware diffusion distillation that allocates model capacity according to changing computational needs. Together, these works illustrate a common principle: efficient models should preserve useful information and allocate computation according to what each task requires, rather than processing everything uniformly.

Finally, I will briefly discuss how these ideas could contribute to more persistent and efficient world models, highlighting open questions around adaptive memory, changing environments, and computation that benefits from accumulated experience.

Erez Sabbag · Amazon · Annapurna Labs Abstract · Bio Advancements in Speculative Decoding

Abstract

ML and HPC workloads now depend on the network as much as on compute — yet standard transport layers were never designed for the cloud, and react poorly to the congestion and link failures that are routine at datacenter scale. This talk explores AWS's answer: SRD (Scalable Reliable Datagram), a multipath transport with latency-based congestion control, implemented natively in the Nitro System. We'll see how it slashes tail latency and recovers from failures in microseconds, and how it powers EFA and massive Trainium2/3 training clusters like Project Rainier.

About the speaker

Erez Sabbag is a Principal Engineer at Annapurna Labs (Amazon Web Services) in Haifa, where he has worked since 2018. Before that, he developed algorithms for flash and NAND storage devices, including error-correcting codes, signal processing, compression and machine-learning methods, at Toshiba, Broadcom and DensBits Technologies (acquired by Broadcom), and he is an inventor on patents in this area. He holds a PhD and an MSc in Electrical Engineering from the Technion, both in information theory, where he also taught courses in digital image processing and information theory.

Mark Silberstein · Technion Abstract · Bio Large-Scale Optics-Only Network for AI Model Training with Low-Radix Optical Switches

Abstract

Machine learning training places immense demands on cluster networks, motivating specialized architectures and co-design with parallelization strategies. Recent designs incorporating optical circuit switches (OCSes) are promising, offering improved cost, power efficiency, and long-term bandwidth scaling than packet switches. However, most existing approaches rely on costly high-radix OCSes and/or combine them with packet switches to achieve competitive performance at scale. Unfortunately, high-radix OCSes are both expensive and slow to reconfigure, limiting both scalability and performance.

We propose Arrays of Cheap Optical Switches (ACOS), which bring application co-design directly to the structure of the reconfigurable fabric. Using low-radix OCSes as building blocks, ACOS supports the forms of reconfiguration needed in training clusters including topology selection, workload adaptation, and failure resilience. The cost of ACOS scales with supported topologies and adaptations rather than with port count, breaking past the scalability barriers of current specialized ML networks. We show through simulation and via our prototype that ACOS-based deployments match the performance of fully provisioned packet-switched networks when training state-of-the-art LLMs at scale, while delivering significant cost savings using existing off-the-shelf OCSes, with strong bandwidth scaling and a fraction of the power of bandwidth-equivalent packet switched networks.

About the speaker

Mark Silberstein is a professor in the Electrical and Computer Engineering Department at the Technion – Israel Institute of Technology, and the head of the Accelerated Computing Systems Lab. His research interests span a broad range of topics in computer systems, including OS, networking, computer architecture, and systems security. He recently spent a sabbatical in NVIDIA Networking, and got particularly excited about emerging networking technologies. He served as a PC co-chair in EuroSys’24 and ASPLOS’26.
